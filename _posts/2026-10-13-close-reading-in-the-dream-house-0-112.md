## Architecture as Memory

Carmen Maria Machado is writing a memoir, but she is very conscious of the fact that memory is constructed. She does not simply tell us what happened; she builds a structure around what happened. That distinction feels central to *In the Dream House*. I am a sucker for architectural metaphors, so the idea of the Dream House immediately worked for me. Machado begins with Louise Bourgeois and the idea that “you pile up associations.” That almost feels like an instruction for how to read the book. The house becomes a memory structure, holding rooms, objects, sensations, fear, desire, shame, and different versions of herself.

Memory does not arrive as one clean narrative.

It arrives fragmented.

A detail here.  
An image there.  
A sentence you suddenly understand differently years later.

Machado builds with those fragments rather than pretending memory is complete. Even the small mention on page 14 that she later buys “a rambling Victorian in Philadelphia” seems to continue the architecture metaphor for anyone paying attention. She leaves one house and eventually occupies another. The Dream House contains the version of herself attached to the relationship, while the Victorian quietly represents the life that continues afterward. Machado does not stop to explain the connection, which is why I think it works. The architecture is already carrying the meaning.

## “You Were Not Always Just a You”

Page 14 makes the split between the narrator and her younger self explicit: “You were not always just a You.” Machado slowly drags out the separation between the person writing the memoir and the person who actually lived through the relationship.

The first person gets to survive.

The second person gets left inside the memory.

That makes her use of “you” more than a stylistic trick. She has created distance between herself now and the version of herself she seems almost embarrassed, frustrated, or even frightened to recognize.

What I find interesting is that she does not completely kill that younger self off. The act of writing brings her back. The narrator wants distance from her, but the memoir itself requires her to keep returning to her.

## Desire and Prolonging the Moment

Page 15 gives us another small clue about the younger Machado. When she first meets the woman, she wants to “prolong” the evening and tries not to stare at her.

That word, **prolong**, feels bigger than the immediate scene.

Machado wants to extend the moment before anything has really happened. She is already investing the encounter with enormous significance. Desire gathers before knowledge does.

She does not know this woman yet, but she is already trying to preserve the feeling.

That connects to the naivety I see throughout these early pages. Machado can become attached to the possibility of something before she fully understands the thing itself.

## Self-Worth, Desire, and Dependence

Page 17 makes that vulnerability more explicit. Looking backward, Machado explains that she distrusted the feelings of boys because she had “no reason to love yourself,” either physically or mentally.

I initially read this almost as Machado trying to explain her sexuality, but I think the more interesting issue is how hard she works to explain **why affection did or did not reach her**. The older Machado is building a psychological explanation for the younger one.

Again, the explanation is almost suspiciously clean.

Low self-worth leads to distrust of gentleness. Intense attention later becomes unusually powerful. That begins to signal the possibility of codependence before the abusive relationship is fully established.

What interests me is not whether this explains why Machado is attracted to women. I do not think the text can prove that. What it does show is someone whose sense of herself seems unusually dependent on how other people see and desire her.

That becomes important later because attention does not simply feel good.

It can become evidence of worth.

And once another person becomes evidence of your worth, leaving that person becomes much harder.

## A Self-Conscious Narrator

Machado is intensely self-conscious, sometimes almost painfully so. She watches herself while also remembering herself. There are constantly two Machados on the page: the woman experiencing the relationship and the writer who already knows what it became. The older narrator recognizes patterns that the younger version often does not.

That gap is interesting, but it also creates one of my frustrations with the book. Up to page 112, Machado often strikes me as remarkably naive. The narrator knows how the story ends, so everything the younger Machado does can be arranged beneath that knowledge. At times I feel sympathy for her. At other times I want her to recognize what seems obvious much earlier.

## Naivety and Repeating Patterns

Some of the experiences surrounding her feel almost stereotypically connected: the difficult relationship with her parents, religion and authority, the religious leader, the desire for acceptance, and then a relationship in which she seems willing to ignore increasingly obvious warning signs.

I do not mean that those experiences automatically produce one another. But Machado herself is constructing a narrative through association, and once she does that, the pattern becomes difficult not to notice. At times, the memoir reads like a person repeatedly handing authority over herself to someone else.

Almost because of this, the genuineness of the memoir can start to come into question for me. The pattern is sometimes almost too perfect. Every piece seems to arrive exactly where it needs to in order to support the larger construction.

That is where the craft begins to peek out.

**Great craftsmen usually hide the work. Here, sometimes I can see the joints.**

That is not necessarily a weakness in the writing. For someone trying to learn the craft, it is actually fascinating. You can see Machado assembling memory, motif, genre, character, and theme into a larger structure.

But there is a tradeoff.

It is great for studying craft.

It can be bad for immersion.

Instead of disappearing completely into Machado's experience, I sometimes find myself studying how effectively she has arranged it. The better the structure becomes, the more aware I become that I am reading a constructed memoir.

## Code-Switching, Form, and Pop Culture

There is also a great deal of code-switching, not simply between languages but between forms of voice. Machado moves between:

memoir,  
criticism,  
fairy tale,  
academic language,  
humor,  
horror,  
second person,  
cultural analysis.

She also establishes her relationship with popular culture almost immediately. On page 16 alone, she moves through references to *Animorphs* and *Pulp Fiction*. Popular culture seems to be part of the vocabulary she uses to understand experience.

The voice changes depending on what she needs the memory to do. Sometimes direct confession seems insufficient, so she reaches for another form or cultural reference. No single genre seems capable of holding the whole experience.

The constant switching is not random. It is another way of showing that the experience cannot be contained by one narrative frame.

## An Uncanny *Watchmen* Resemblance

Some of Machado's handling of memory, desire, and time made me immediately think of Alan Moore's *Watchmen*, particularly the material surrounding Jon Osterman and Janey Slater before the accident.

I think the resemblance is uncanny enough that I wonder whether Machado lifted, adapted, or at least absorbed part of that technique. I cannot demonstrate direct borrowing, so I would not state it as fact, but as a reader I find the parallel difficult to dismiss as merely generic.

Both works stretch a relationship across time so that the reader experiences the beginning while already feeling the ending sitting beside it.

**Past and future begin occupying the same emotional space.**

An intimate moment is not allowed to exist only as it was experienced then. Later knowledge reaches backward and changes the emotional weight of the earlier memory.

The pop culture references appearing as early as page 16 make this possibility more interesting to me. They do not prove a *Watchmen* influence, but they establish that Machado is comfortable building her memoir through cultural material.

Whether deliberate, unconscious, or simply an uncanny parallel, the comparison helped me recognize something important about her method.

She lets later knowledge contaminate earlier memory.

The beginning cannot simply be the beginning anymore.

## Gender and Roles

The gender dynamic is also interesting because Machado does not necessarily dismantle the traditional roles of abuse. There is still an aggressor and a victim, still dominance and vulnerability.

What changes is **who can occupy those roles**.

The memoir shows, or at least tries to show, that masculinity does not automatically belong to the aggressor, just as femininity does not automatically mean victimhood. Anyone can occupy either position regardless of gender.

There also seems to be uncertainty in the younger Machado about identity, sexuality, relationships, and what intimacy is supposed to look like. I would hesitate to diagnose that uncertainty beyond what she actually tells us, but it contributes to the sense that she is still constructing herself while entering the relationship.

Gender does not erase the traditional power dynamic. Machado keeps the aggressor and victim structure intact but separates those roles from gender.

## Where I Resist Machado

This is where my reaction becomes more complicated. I admire Machado's writing and the structure of the memoir. I understand what she is trying to construct. But I frequently find the narrator whiny and, at moments, almost insufferable.

Part of that may come from my own experience. I have lived through situations where pushing forward was not really optional, where mortality itself came into question and survival required action rather than prolonged interpretation.

That changes how I respond to Machado.

I keep wanting her to move, to decide, to become harder, and to stop allowing the Dream House to define the limits of her world.

## Trading Who You Are for Who You Want to Be

One idea kept coming back to me while reading:

**At some point, you have to be willing to trade who you are for who you want to become.**

That does not mean pretending pain did not happen. It means accepting that survival may require abandoning parts of yourself that once felt essential: habits, dependencies, ways of thinking, even identities.

This is where I am not convinced by Machado, at least not yet.

Up through page 112, she appears extraordinarily good at analyzing herself, but I am less convinced that she is capable of transforming herself. She can explain what happened to her and the patterns that shaped it, but I do not yet believe she can fully move beyond them.

## Understanding Versus Changing

That distinction between understanding and changing may be the question I care about most.

Machado seems to believe that naming and reconstructing the experience carries power, and I agree to a point.

But self-awareness is not transformation.

You can understand exactly why you became someone and still remain that person.

That is the tension I am carrying into the second half.

## How Machado Weaves the Strands

The craft is strongest when these ideas begin overlapping. Memory becomes narrative. Narrative becomes genre. Genre becomes a method of understanding trauma.

The Dream House works because it can hold all of those meanings at once without becoming only one thing. Machado keeps adding associations until the image becomes large enough to hold the experience.

That brings me back to Bourgeois:

**You pile up associations.**

Machado does exactly that.

## Question to Carry Forward

The question I have after page 112 is no longer simply, “How does Machado escape the Dream House?”

It is whether she can become someone who no longer needs the Dream House to explain who she is.

For me, that is the real test of closure.

Remembering is not enough.

Understanding is not enough.

At some point, you have to decide whether the structure that explains your past is still allowed to define your future.
