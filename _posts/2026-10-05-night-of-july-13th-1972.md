*“I Was Born” — a writing exercise.*

I was born on the night of July 13th, 1972.

It was the summer of 1972, three years removed from sixty-nine.

The air was thick; one could cut it like fine cheese.

As I reached toward the corpse, I pried my ascot from James’s dead, gripped fingers.

The last time I had seen it, we had just watched Santana perform “Soul Sacrifice.”

James, Mary, Ricardo, and Taylor were all there.

We had always gotten ourselves into the good kind of trouble. That night... was not one of those nights.

It had taken years, but I finally escaped the trouble of that night.

James’s frayed leather vest was still intact, its smell overpowering the death musk that escaped from below the departed.

As I gently touched my departed friend—

A click pierced the air.

“FREEZE, ASSHOLE!”

I instinctively swung the shovel around.

The unmistakable sound of metal splitting a grapefruit. A new mist had pierced the air.

That night was forty-eight years ago.

As I tuck my youngest daughter into bed, I often wonder if I had truly escaped that night.

My identity dead, a facade alive.

It had felt as if a guardian had been watching over us....

On the counter, my wife had laid out the treasures my daughter had found in our woods.

Among them were strips from a frayed jacket and an unforgettable smell.

I was born on the night of July 13th, 1972.

The last time I saw him, he was wearing the ascot. Red. No, orange, though in the dark, it looked black.

We had spent the day sweating through our shirts and pretending we were younger than we were.

Santana had played “Soul Sacrifice,” and for a little while, there was nothing in the world but drums, heat, beer, and the people beside you.

Mary was laughing. Ricardo had lost his shoes. Taylor kept saying we should go home.

None of us did.

He stood under the yellow light with the ascot loose around his neck.

“You look ridiculous,” I told him.

He smiled. That was the last normal thing I remember.

Later, the woods were quiet.

Not peaceful. Quiet.

There is a difference.

The ground was wet beneath my knees. My leather vest stuck to my back.

Somewhere nearby, someone was breathing too hard.

Maybe me. Maybe him. I had the ascot in my hand. I don't remember taking it.

I remember looking down at it, then at him, then behind him.

There was something between the trees.

Not a person. Not exactly.

It had been there before. I knew that somehow.

Watching. Waiting.

I tried to say his name, but nothing came out. He looked at me as though I had already told him everything.

Then the night folded in on itself.

For years afterward, I thought that was the end of it. It wasn't.

Some things do not end when you die. They simply stop belonging to you.

He came back eventually. Older. Different. A shovel in his hands.

I could smell the leather before I saw him. Funny what remains. Not faces. Not voices. Smells.

The inside of an old car. Rain on hot pavement. Leather after forty-eight summers.

He reached toward me.

Toward the ascot.

I wanted to tell him not to. I wanted to tell him to leave it there.

Leave me there. Leave all of us there.

But the dead are poor conversationalists.

His fingers touched mine. A click came from behind him.

Metal. Small. Final.

Someone shouted. He turned.

The shovel moved. Then came the sound.

Wet and hard. Like splitting fruit.

For one second, the woods were young again.

Then the mist settled. Years passed.

His daughter found the jacket. Or pieces of it.

Children are always finding things adults were wise enough to bury.

She carried them home. Her mother put them on the counter.

And somewhere in that house, he smelled the leather.

I know because, for the first time in forty-eight years, I smelled him too.

---

I was born on the night of July 13th, 1972.

It was his death that had sent me.

And when the man with the shovel finally disappeared between the trees,

I followed.

When I became aware again, I was no longer where I had been.

I was beneath it. Around it. Inside the wet earth.

The man with the shovel stood over what remained.

He looked frightened. I did not know his name. I did not know mine.

But I knew him…
