## Question to Carry Forward

The question I have after page 112 is no longer simply, “How does Machado escape the Dream House?” It is whether she can become someone who no longer needs the Dream House to explain who she is. For me, that is the real test of closure.

Remembering is not enough. Understanding is not enough.

At some point, you have to decide whether the structure that explains your past is still allowed to define your future.

## Naming What Happened

The second half becomes less about simply recounting the relationship and more about whether Machado can properly name what happened. In “Dream House as Naming the Animals,” naming becomes its own problem. Machado is trying to describe queer domestic abuse while also arguing that the cultural language around it is incomplete.

That helps explain the form of the book. Memoir alone is not enough, so she reaches for criticism, history, folklore, pop culture, legal cases, and formal experiments. She is not just telling us what happened. She is trying to find the language that makes what happened recognizable.

## Ambiguity Might Be the Whole Book

“Dream House as Ambiguity” could almost describe the entire memoir. Machado keeps occupying contradictory positions at once. The relationship is loving and abusive. She is intelligent and naive. She recognizes warning signs and ignores them. She wants autonomy while repeatedly surrendering pieces of it. She remembers certain moments with extraordinary specificity while admitting that other parts are missing.

The ambiguity is not something the book eventually solves. It is the condition the book lives inside.

## Sexuality Is Messier Than the Retrospective Story

Machado sometimes writes as though the older narrator can now explain the younger Machado very cleanly, but the actual history is messier. She describes attraction to women, but she also describes relationships and sex with men. Having sex with men is, at the level of behavior, heterosexual activity, even if behavior does not automatically determine identity.

That distinction matters. The younger Machado seems less settled than the older narrator occasionally makes her appear. She is figuring out desire while living it. This is part of why I resist any overly clean retrospective explanation of her sexuality. The life happened first. The labels and explanations came later.

## Masculine and Feminine Energy

The memoir also keeps staging a conflict between masculine and feminine energies, although those roles are not neatly attached to male and female bodies. There is aggression and submission, pursuit and retreat, control and accommodation, dominance and dependence.

The relationship does not eliminate the traditional aggressor and victim structure. It shows that those positions are not biologically fixed. Anyone can occupy either role. That may be one of the more important things Machado demonstrates without needing to overstate it.

## Human Experience Is Still Human

This is also where I sometimes resist how heavily Machado emphasizes difference. Queer history matters. Representation matters. The legal and cultural treatment of same-sex relationships clearly changes the context. But many of the emotional mechanics Machado describes are universal.

People want to be loved. They become dependent, ignore warning signs, manipulate one another, fear abandonment, and sometimes stay when they know they should leave. The parts of the people involved change the social context, but they do not completely change the emotional machinery.

People feel.

That does not make Machado's story less specifically queer. If anything, it makes the story more human.

## Co-Dependence

More than anything, I keep seeing co-dependence. Machado wants desire to confirm her value. Earlier relationships already show how intensely she wants someone to want her, and in the abusive relationship that need becomes dangerous.

She sees the red flags, gives ultimatums, and recognizes the behavior, but she stays. The clearest example is therapy. Machado tells the woman to get help or she will leave. The woman goes, then stops. Machado knows the boundary has been crossed, yet she never raises it again.

That is the distinction I kept coming back to in the first half: **knowing versus acting**.

Her problem is not simply that she cannot identify what is happening. She cannot separate herself from the person doing it.

## Unreliable Narrator

“Dream House as Unreliable Narrator” finally confronts a question hanging over the entire memoir: can we trust her?

Machado remembers being called melodramatic and a “drama queen,” then moves from that personal history into a broader argument about girls being taught that their perspectives are unreliable. This is where I push back. That experience may be true for Machado and for many other women, but not every girl is raised that way. Machado sometimes takes something intensely personal and moves quickly toward universality.

That does not make the argument false. It does make me wonder whether the larger cultural argument occasionally protects the individual narrator from criticism.

The title works in two directions. Machado is asking whether she can trust herself, but she is also anticipating the possibility that we might not trust her. The memoir keeps defending itself while telling itself.

## Proof and the Body

That question becomes even stronger in “Dream House as Proof.” Machado imagines what evidence could actually prove what happened: recordings, witnesses, photographs, physical reactions, memory. The problem is that psychological and emotional abuse rarely leave the kind of evidence people expect.

So Machado moves the trial somewhere else: the court of other people, the court of queer history, and eventually the court of the body.

This develops the central metaphor in an interesting way. At the beginning, memory lives inside architecture. Here, memory begins living physically inside Machado. Her cells change and her body changes, but the nervous system remembers.

The body becomes an archive.

## The Missing Memory

“Dream House as Soap Opera” makes the limits of memory explicit when the woman tells Machado that she does not remember part of what happened. That blank space matters because the memoir is otherwise filled with extraordinary specificity. Suddenly, memory disappears.

Oddly, this makes the book more convincing to me. Machado does not pretend every room of the Dream House remains accessible. Some doors are locked. Some rooms are gone. The memoir is not proof in the traditional sense. It is evidence assembled from what remains.

## The Cliché Problem

“Dream House as Cliché” directly answers one of my criticisms from the first half. Machado acknowledges that abuse can sound almost absurdly predictable: the threat, the apology, the good period, the violence, the reconciliation, and then the cycle begins again.

The familiarity can flatten the experience until the reader stops actually seeing it. This is interesting because I had almost the same problem with the memoir itself. Earlier, I questioned whether some of Machado's patterns felt too perfect. Family problems, insecurity, dependence, warning signs, abuse, and retrospective understanding sometimes line up so neatly that the construction becomes visible.

Machado seems to know this. Sometimes reality really is clichéd.

That complicates my criticism without completely eliminating it.

**Great craftsmen usually hide the work. Here, sometimes I can see the joints.**

The difference in the second half is that Machado occasionally points directly at the joints herself.

## The Red Flags and the Problem of Action

The therapy section is where my frustration with the younger Machado becomes strongest. She gives the woman an ultimatum: get help or I leave. The woman goes to therapy, then stops. Machado remembers the promise she made to herself and knows exactly what has happened.

Then she does not enforce it.

There are so many red flags that the question is no longer whether Machado can see them. She can. The question is why seeing them still does not produce action.

Again, understanding is not transformation.

## Pop Culture as Memory

Machado keeps using other stories to understand her own. *Star Trek*, music, film, folklore, and other books become interpretive tools. “Dream House as Five Lights” is especially useful because it turns gaslighting into something visible. One person insists reality is different until another person begins doubting what they can see.

Machado also openly discusses pastiche and material being lightly lifted from other works. That does not prove my earlier suspicion about *Watchmen*, but it makes that kind of influence completely plausible. Machado thinks intertextually. Other people's stories become part of how she organizes her own.

Past and present occupy the same emotional space, but so do her memories and the stories she has consumed.

## The Choose Your Own Adventure Trap

The choose-your-own-adventure section may be the best formal experiment in the book. Machado gives the reader choices, but the choices keep circling back into the same situation.

Eventually I started skipping ahead because I was tired of it.

Then Machado caught me.

The page essentially tells the reader that they should not be there. They cheated because they got sick of the cycle and wanted out. That is excellent craft because the reader's frustration becomes part of the meaning. You think you are choosing, but every path returns you to the same structure. The only real way out is to stop following the structure.

That mirrors the relationship itself. The form does not simply describe entrapment. It creates it.

At the same time, this brings me back to my continuing criticism of Machado. I can see exactly what she is doing. The mechanism is brilliant, but it is visible. For someone studying writing, this is fascinating. For immersion, it can be distracting.

The craft and the story compete for my attention.

## Remembering Yourself at Your Worst

The Sarah Manguso quotation near the beginning of Part IV also feels central. The problem with people seeing you at your worst is not only that they remember. It is that **you remember**.

That connects directly to Machado's use of second person. The “you” is not simply a literary trick. It is a version of herself she has to keep watching.

Maybe that is why the younger Machado can sometimes seem frustrating even to the narrator herself. Machado is not merely asking us to watch this person make bad decisions. She has to watch herself make them again.

The problem is not only other people's judgment. It is being unable to escape your own memory of yourself.

## Trust

Near the end, Machado describes watching a deer and says the intimacy would have been almost unbearable if she thought it was trust. I like the hesitation. Maybe the animal trusts her. Maybe it does not. Maybe she is projecting meaning onto it.

That uncertainty feels more mature than some of the earlier sections because Machado finally allows something to remain unresolved. Not everything needs to become proof or a perfect explanation.

## Closure: From House to Body

The ending changes my reading of the entire architecture metaphor. Earlier, Machado tells us that “you were not always just a You.” I read the “I” as the surviving narrator and the “you” as the younger Machado trapped inside memory.

At the end, Machado wishes that she had always lived in her current body and that the younger “you” could have lived there with her.

That is the closure.

The book begins by putting memory inside a house. It ends by putting the self inside the body. That is a significant movement.

I spent much of the first half wanting Machado to trade who she was for who she wanted to become. The ending offers something slightly different. She does not destroy the former self. She absorbs her. The “I” and the “you” can finally occupy the same body.

Maybe transformation does not require killing the former version of yourself. Maybe it requires becoming strong enough to carry that person without allowing her to control you.

That is more convincing to me than simply tearing the Dream House down.

But it also proves part of my original point. Integration is not necessarily the same thing as resolving trauma. Machado understands the past enormously well by the end of the book, but I am less certain that the book proves she has fully worked through it.

Maybe writing the book is how she does that work. The memoir itself may be the act of confronting, organizing, and containing the experience.

## Does the Ending Earn It?

Mostly.

The ending works because Machado finally stops explaining quite so much. The body, the moon, the landscape, the animals, and the younger self are allowed to coexist without another enormous theoretical structure being placed over them.

But I still see the craftsmanship. The emotional landing is extremely precise, sometimes almost too precise. That tension never completely disappears for me.

Machado is an exceptional craftsman.

Sometimes I wish she would let me forget that.

## The Afterword Could Have Been the Preface

Pages 245 through 250 could almost function as a preface. Machado finally explains the research problem directly: queer domestic abuse has a fragmented archive, relevant stories are difficult to find, terminology is complicated, and she has deliberately chosen a specific rhetorical and historical lens.

Academically, that material could come first. It would immediately tell us what the book is trying to accomplish.

Narratively, I think that would be a mistake.

If Machado placed those pages at the beginning, she would give us the blueprint before letting us enter the house. Instead, we walk through the rooms first. Only afterward does she show us how the structure was built.

That is why the Afterword belongs where it is, even though it could function perfectly well as an introduction.

A preface would explain the house.

An afterword lets us tour it first.

## What Closure Actually Means

The second half changes my answer to the central question I carried from pages 0 through 112. I originally wondered whether Machado could become someone who no longer needed the Dream House to explain who she was.

By the end, I think she gets closer.

Not because the past disappears, because she solves every contradiction, or because the trauma is obviously gone. Closure comes from changing her relationship to the memory. The house stops being somewhere she simply lives. It becomes somewhere she can deliberately return to, examine, and write about.

This also makes the recurring architecture metaphor much more apparent. Machado needs the house because trauma is too large and fragmented to hold as one clean memory. She gives it rooms, doors, hallways, and different ways of entering so she can move through it piece by piece. The memoir becomes the house she builds around the experience. Writing it allows her to enter those rooms deliberately, rather than simply being trapped inside them. She may not fully resolve the trauma, but she can finally move through its architecture and, at least narratively, find the door out.
